\rcomment{Reviewer 2, Comment 2}{In section 3.3.4, a constant efficiency $\eta$ is used to compute average electrical power from average mechanical power. This appears to be a main limitation, as it is highlighted in a number of studies on loss-aware control of WECs that a constant efficiency may not be accurate for either power evaluation or control, given the oscillating operation of WECs. While an $\eta$ is selected to match a specific operating condition, the efficiency may change when the motion amplitude changes.}
\response We thank the reviewer for raising this important point, and we agree that a constant efficiency cannot capture how losses vary with motion amplitude. In MDOcean, the main amplitude-dependent losses are modeled explicitly rather than through $\eta$. The generator includes a winding impedance (Appendix~C.2.4), so ohmic and inductive effects vary with the current and therefore with the motion amplitude, and power is maximized at the generator's electrical port rather than its mechanical port. The constant efficiency $\eta$ accounts only for the remaining losses, such as those in the electrical drive; its value of 80\% was chosen to match the assumption in the RM3 reference report (Neary et al., 2014b) for the comparisons in Section~4. \fillin{We have clarified this in Section 3.3.6 (p.~X, lines~A-B) and added amplitude-dependent drive losses to the future work in Section 5.3 and Table~11.}

However, the third part also addresses a specific constraint (force limit). I would suggest clarifying what specific constraints these two parts are intended to handle and using a clearer terminology.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this suggestion.
We have renamed the two parts as follows:

Section 3.3.3 adds a section \textit{Method Choice for Each Nonlinearity} (p.~\pageref{aor:sec:mfd-method-choice}) to explain why different constraint-handling methods (M1--M5) are used for different types of constraints.
The text reads,
\begin{quote}
For the control force limit, the corresponding describing function (saturated-sine) is simple to implement, and prior \gls{acr-rm3} optimizations \citep{aor:mccabe_multidisciplinary_2022,aor:gaebele_tpl_2025,aor:mcgilton_optimal_2024} show the constraint to be frequently active and impact results strongly, justifying the use of M1.2 (full \gls{acr-qlps}).
For amplitude limits in the operational design load case, M1.2 produces the same result as M2 due to the low-pass assumption that makes the constrained quantity a pure sinusoid, so we use the simpler M2 without loss of optimality.

For a power limit, under cases where the power limit is the only constraint, applying M2 is straightforward, and applying M1.2 is possible but more cumbersome:
assuming that the optimal power signal is a saturated sinusoid implies that the corresponding force signal is the quotient of the truncated power sinusoid and the amplitude sinusoid, which requires deriving an additional describing function.
When a power limit is applied simultaneously with a force limit, both methods break down.
M2 would be incompatible with the previous choice of M1.2 for the co-occurring force limit, and M1.2 creates ambiguity about the nonlinear signal shapes,
since it is not possible for the force and power signals to both be saturated sinusoids while assuming sinusoidal amplitude.
To avoid this additional complexity, the power limit uses M3, although future work should consider the use of M1.2 or M2 to improve accuracy and optimality.

For slamming amplitude limits in the storm design load case, approach M5 is actually the only option. 
This is because the device is already in survival mode (preventing method M4), is not producing power (preventing methods M3 and M4), and is not applying a control input (preventing methods M1 and M2).
\end{quote}

Since the only dynamic nonlinearity handled using method M1.2 is the force limit, the force limit is the only constraint discussed in the ``Constrained, Nonlinear Control (M1.2)'' section.

\rcomment{Reviewer 2, Comment 4}{In section 3.3.5 the ``Constrained'' part, before (18), ``Each metric is written as a quadratic product of the port variable vector with a matrix''. Is this valid for all metrics that may need to be constrained? If a WEC velocity/displacement constraint is to be applied, should the constrained metric be the port variable itself, rather than its quadratic function? This goes back the problem of variable definition.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this question.
Because the complex form of the port variable is used as the decision variable (e.g. $\hat{q}$), the magnitude of the port variable $|\hat{q}|$ is not a linear expression of the decision variable, it is the square root of the variable multiplied by its complex conjugate:
\begin{equation}
 |\hat{q}| = \sqrt{\hat{q}^* \hat{q}}
\end{equation}
Squaring both sides, we see that a limit on the magnitude of a port variable, such as a velocity or displacement limit $|\hat{q}| \le q_{max}$, is equivalent to the quadratic constraint $\hat{q}^* \hat{q} \le q_{max}^2$, which has the form of Eq.~(17) with the matrix given in Table~6.
Any constraint on a linear combination of active power, reactive power, and port variable magnitude can be formulated as a quadratic constraint.
The only metric that is commonly constrained that cannot be written in quadratic form is peak power, which is fourth-order (Eq.~(18)) and is treated separately in Appendix C.3.1.
We have added this clarification to \Cref{aor:sec:optimal-control}
(p.~\pageref{aor:lne:quadratic-metrics-start}, lines~\ref{aor:lne:quadratic-metrics-start}--\ref{aor:lne:quadratic-metrics-end}).

We agree that the original manuscript did not make its novel contributions clear enough, and \fillin{we have revised the Abstract (lines~A-B), the contributions in Section 1.4 (lines~C--D), and the Introduction (new objectives paragraph, lines~E-F)} to state them explicitly. Beyond integrating the models, the work contributes:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The quasi-linear pseudo-spectral (QLPS) formulation, which extends frequency-domain constraint handling to include nonlinear dynamics. To our knowledge, it is the first use of describing functions for both controller synthesis and evaluation in wave energy (Section~3.3.2).
  \item A closed-form solution of the constrained optimal control problem, posed as a univariate quadratically-constrained quadratic program with a geometric interpretation on the complex reflection-coefficient plane. This problem previously required numerical optimization (Section~3.3.7, Appendix~C.3.1).
  \item An analytical scaling law for annual average power as a function of the constraint limits, with conditions for its convexity (Eq.~(35), Appendix~C.3.2).
  \item Findings that are only visible because the disciplines are coupled: at the LCOE optimum, every constraint is active in at least one sea state; the minimum-LCOE force limit lies far below the unsaturated solution (Figure~20); and the damping-plate fatigue constraint splits the feasible design space into separate regions (Figure~21).
\end{enumerate}
We hope these revisions make the scientific contribution of the work clearer.
