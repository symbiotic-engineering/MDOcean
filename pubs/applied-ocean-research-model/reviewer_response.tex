\rcomment{Reviewer 2, Comment 2}{In section 3.3.4, a constant efficiency $\eta$ is used to compute average electrical power from average mechanical power. This appears to be a main limitation, as it is highlighted in a number of studies on loss-aware control of WECs that a constant efficiency may not be accurate for either power evaluation or control, given the oscillating operation of WECs. While an $\eta$ is selected to match a specific operating condition, the efficiency may change when the motion amplitude changes.}
\response We thank the reviewer for raising this important point, and we agree that a constant efficiency cannot capture how losses vary with motion amplitude. In MDOcean, the main amplitude-dependent losses are modeled explicitly rather than through $\eta$. The generator includes a winding impedance (Appendix~C.2.4), so ohmic and inductive effects vary with the current and therefore with the motion amplitude, and power is maximized at the generator's electrical port rather than its mechanical port. The constant efficiency $\eta$ accounts only for the remaining losses, such as those in the electrical drive; its value of 80\% was chosen to match the assumption in the RM3 reference report (Neary et al., 2014b) for the comparisons in Section~4. \fillin{We have clarified this in Section 3.3.6 (p.~X, lines~A-B) and added amplitude-dependent drive losses to the future work in Section 5.3 and Table~11.}

Section 3.3.3 adds a section \textit{Method Choice for Each Nonlinearity} (p.~\pageref{aor:sec:mfd-method-choice}) to explain why different constraint-handling methods (M1--M5) are used for different types of constraints.
The text reads,
\begin{quote}
For the control force limit, the corresponding describing function (saturated-sine) is simple to implement, and prior \gls{acr-rm3} optimizations \citep{aor:mccabe_multidisciplinary_2022,aor:gaebele_tpl_2025,aor:mcgilton_optimal_2024} show the constraint to be frequently active and impact results strongly, justifying the use of M1.2 (full \gls{acr-qlps}).
For amplitude limits in the operational design load case, M1.2 produces the same result as M2 due to the low-pass assumption that makes the constrained quantity a pure sinusoid, so we use the simpler M2 without loss of optimality.

For a power limit, under cases where the power limit is the only constraint, applying M2 is straightforward, and applying M1.2 is possible but more cumbersome:
assuming that the optimal power signal is a saturated sinusoid implies that the corresponding force signal is the quotient of the truncated power sinusoid and the amplitude sinusoid, which requires deriving an additional describing function.
When a power limit is applied simultaneously with a force limit, both methods break down.
M2 would be incompatible with the previous choice of M1.2 for the co-occurring force limit, and M1.2 creates ambiguity about the nonlinear signal shapes,
since it is not possible for the force and power signals to both be saturated sinusoids while assuming sinusoidal amplitude.
To avoid this additional complexity, the power limit uses M3, although future work should consider the use of M1.2 or M2 to improve accuracy and optimality.

For slamming amplitude limits in the storm design load case, approach M5 is actually the only option. 
This is because the device is already in survival mode (preventing method M4), is not producing power (preventing methods M3 and M4), and is not applying a control input (preventing methods M1 and M2).
\end{quote}

Since the only dynamic nonlinearity handled using method M1.2 is the force limit, the force limit is the only constraint discussed in the ``Constrained, Nonlinear Control (M1.2)'' section.

\rcomment{Reviewer 2, Comment 4}{In section 3.3.5 the ``Constrained'' part, before (18), ``Each metric is written as a quadratic product of the port variable vector with a matrix''. Is this valid for all metrics that may need to be constrained? If a WEC velocity/displacement constraint is to be applied, should the constrained metric be the port variable itself, rather than its quadratic function? This goes back the problem of variable definition.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this question.
Because the complex form of the port variable is used as the decision variable (e.g. $\hat{q}$), the magnitude of the port variable $|\hat{q}|$ is not a linear expression of the decision variable, it is the square root of the variable multiplied by its complex conjugate:
\begin{equation}
 |\hat{q}| = \sqrt{\hat{q}^* \hat{q}}
\end{equation}
Squaring both sides, we see that a limit on the magnitude of a port variable, such as a velocity or displacement limit $|\hat{q}| \le q_{max}$, is equivalent to the quadratic constraint $\hat{q}^* \hat{q} \le q_{max}^2$, which has the form of Eq.~(17) with the matrix given in Table~6.
Any constraint on a linear combination of active power, reactive power, and port variable magnitude can be formulated as a quadratic constraint.
The only metric that is commonly constrained that cannot be written in quadratic form is peak power, which is fourth-order (Eq.~(18)) and is treated separately in Appendix C.3.1.
We have added this clarification to \Cref{aor:sec:optimal-control}
(p.~\pageref{aor:lne:quadratic-metrics-start}, lines~\ref{aor:lne:quadratic-metrics-start}--\ref{aor:lne:quadratic-metrics-end}).

\rcomment{Reviewer 2, Comment 6}{In section 3.3.5 and the corresponding appendix, I did not fully understand how the force saturation is applied. Are you simply scale the force limit by 4/pi and apply it on the force coefficient?}
\response We thank the reviewer for this question. 
No. The force limit is not simply scaled by $4/\pi$ and then imposed directly on the force coefficient. The nonlinear controller first applies the force saturation in the time domain. The saturated sinusoidal force is then represented by its fundamental Fourier component, whose amplitude is used in the frequency-domain balance. The resulting amplitude-dependent effective control gains $B_l$ and $K_l$ enter the linearized frequency-domain dynamics. The factor $4/\pi$ is the limiting fundamental-to-peak ratio for a fully saturated sinusoidal input, i.e., the square-wave limit; it is not a universal correction to the force limit. The full describing-function derivation and the point at which this factor appears are given in Appendix~C.1.

\rcomment{Reviewer 2, Comment 7}{In section 3.3.6, equation (24), are these coefficients fixed, or do they depend on the motion amplitude?}
\response We thank the reviewer for this question. The drag coefficients $\mathbf{B}_d$ and $\vec{\gamma}_d$ in this equation (now Eq.~(23) in Section~3.3.8) are not fixed. As shown in Eq.~(98) of Appendix~C.4, they depend on both the amplitude and the phase of the body velocity. Because the coefficients depend on the response they help determine, MDOcean solves for them by fixed-point iteration, which typically converges in five to eight iterations to within 0.01~m and 3$^\circ$ across all sea states (Appendix~C.4.3). \fillin{We have added this clarification to Section 3.3.8 (p.~X, lines~A-B): ``The drag coefficients depend on the amplitude and phase of the body velocity (Eq.~(98)) and are therefore converged by fixed-point iteration, typically within five to eight iterations (Appendix~C.4.3).''}

\rcomment{Reviewer 2, Comment 8}{Finally, in section 4, while you have presented validation results on the dynamic model against WEC-Sim, please can you discuss more on the validation of the optimal control solution against an ideal benchmark such as MPC? Given that the proposed framework has a number of approximate treatments in constraint handling, nonlinearities handling, as well as the monochromatic simplification of irregular waves, the accuracy of each assumption, as well as how much they affect the final evaluation result, remains unclear.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. We agree that comparison with an ideal benchmark such as model predictive control would be valuable for assessing the combined effect of the approximations. The WEC-Sim comparisons in Figure~12 and Appendix~C.8 provide evidence for the dynamics formulation under the stated matched assumptions, but they do not constitute a comprehensive validation of every approximation in the complete optimization workflow.

\section*{Reviewer 3}

\rcomment{Reviewer 3, Comment 1}{I believe the proposed framework will be useful from the practical viewpoint, but, on the other hand, from the viewpoint of a research journal paper, I cannot find any innovative new findings or developments worth being published as a journal paper.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this assessment and for recognizing the practical value of the framework. We agree that the original manuscript did not make its novel contributions clear enough, and \fillin{we have revised the Abstract (lines~A-B), the contributions in Section 1.4 (lines~C--D), and the Introduction (new objectives paragraph, lines~E-F)} to state them explicitly. Beyond integrating the models, the work contributes:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The quasi-linear pseudo-spectral (QLPS) formulation, which extends frequency-domain constraint handling to include nonlinear dynamics. To our knowledge, it is the first use of describing functions for both controller synthesis and evaluation in wave energy (Section~3.3.2).
  \item A closed-form solution of the constrained optimal control problem, posed as a univariate quadratically-constrained quadratic program with a geometric interpretation on the complex reflection-coefficient plane. This problem previously required numerical optimization (Section~3.3.7, Appendix~C.3.1).
  \item An analytical scaling law for annual average power as a function of the constraint limits, with conditions for its convexity (Eq.~(35), Appendix~C.3.2).
  \item Findings that are only visible because the disciplines are coupled: at the LCOE optimum, every constraint is active in at least one sea state; the minimum-LCOE force limit lies far below the unsaturated solution (Figure~20); and the damping-plate fatigue constraint splits the feasible design space into separate regions (Figure~21).
\end{enumerate}
We hope these revisions make the scientific contribution of the work clearer.

\section*{Reviewer 4}

We thank Reviewer~4 for the thorough and constructive review, and for noting the relevance of the work, the usefulness of the integrated framework, the interest of the quasi-linearized pseudo-spectral formulation, and the value of its computational speed for design-space exploration.

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, General Comment}{My main concern is not the general value of the framework, but the way its accuracy and validation are presented. Some results appear to be calibrated to the RM3 reference data using scaling factors, while other parts are compared against WEC-Sim under matched modelling assumptions. These are both useful exercises, but they should not be presented in the same way as independent validation. The authors should more clearly distinguish validation, calibration, verification and benchmarking, and should moderate the claims about model accuracy and applicability.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this careful comment, and we agree. We have revised Section~4 to use these terms consistently. \textit{Verification} refers to checks of the numerical implementation, including MEEM convergence and comparison of hydrodynamic coefficients with WAMIT results. \textit{Validation} refers to the comparison of the dynamics module with WEC-Sim under matched modeling assumptions and without tuning to the WEC-Sim results. \textit{Calibration} refers specifically to the force, power, and structural-mass scaling factors used to reproduce the RM3 reference data. \textit{Benchmarking} refers to the runtime comparisons with other computational approaches. We have also renamed and reorganized the relevant subsection(s) in Section~4 ([revised Section~4 subsection titles: page/line reference]) and moderated accuracy and applicability claims in the Abstract ([abstract accuracy/scope statement: page/line reference]), Section~4 ([validation/calibration claims: page/line reference]), and Conclusion ([conclusion scope statement: page/line reference]).

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 1}{The comparison with WEC-Sim is useful for assessing the dynamics module under matched assumptions. However, the agreement with the RM3 reference model partly relies on scaling factors for force, power and structural mass. This should be described as calibration rather than independent validation. The authors should clearly state which quantities are predicted without tuning and which quantities are matched after applying scaling factors.}
\response We thank the reviewer and agree. We have renamed the RM3 comparison as calibration and now distinguish calibrated quantities from quantities predicted without tuning. Table~9 reports the errors before and after application of the scaling factors, making clear which agreement results from calibration. The WEC-Sim comparison remains the primary independent validation of the dynamics module under matched assumptions. Please see also our response to the General Comment.

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 2}{The manuscript applies scaling factors to bring storm force, average power and structural mass into agreement with the reference model. This is reasonable for reproducing RM3, but it raises an important question: how would these factors be selected for a new WEC design without reference data? The authors should discuss this point and, if possible, show the main validation errors before and after applying the scaling factors.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this question. Table~9 now reports the principal errors before and after applying the RM3 scaling factors. These factors are calibration parameters, not universal constants that can be transferred to an arbitrary new WEC design. For a new design, their use would require an appropriate higher-fidelity or experimental reference for the corresponding quantity; without such reference data, the uncalibrated model should be treated as a screening model rather than as a calibrated prediction. We have clarified this distinction and moderated the associated accuracy claims.

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 3}{Including structural checks is an important strength of the framework. However, the structural module is semi-analytical, and FEA-based validation is still listed as future work. The manuscript should avoid giving the impression that the structural model is already suitable for detailed structural design or certification. It is better presented as an early-stage screening tool.}
\response We thank the reviewer and agree. MDOcean is intended as an early-stage screening tool for exploring large design spaces, not for detailed structural design or certification. \fillin{We have stated this explicitly in Section 3.4 (lines~A-B), Section 5.3 (lines~C-D), and the Conclusion (lines~E-F), and revised the Conclusion's description of the structures module accordingly.} FEA-based validation of the structural module remains future work (Section~5.3 and Table~11).

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 4}{The use of equivalent regular waves and linear wave theory for storm cases is a major approximation. This can miss nonlinear wave effects and transient load peaks. The authors acknowledge this limitation, but the main text and conclusions should make it clearer that storm-load and survivability predictions are preliminary and should not replace higher-fidelity analyses.}
\response We thank the reviewer and agree. We have clarified that the storm-load and survivability calculations are preliminary screening estimates based on the stated regular-wave and linear-wave assumptions and are not substitutes for higher-fidelity analysis of nonlinear wave loading, transient peaks, or certification. This qualification is now stated in Section~3.3.10 ([storm-load limitation: page/line reference]), Section~5.3 ([limitation/future-work statement: page/line reference]), and the Conclusion ([storm-load scope statement: page/line reference]).

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 5}{The use of a JPD is useful for long-term performance estimates, but the dynamics are still based on equivalent regular-wave approximations. The authors should discuss how this affects annual average power, fatigue-related quantities and controller performance, especially for broad-banded sea states.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. The equivalent-regular-wave approximation affects these quantities differently. For annual average power, the joint probability distribution still provides a long-term weighting of sea states, but the response within each sea state is represented by an equivalent regular-wave response rather than a full irregular-wave time history. For fatigue, this is a more substantial limitation because a single-frequency representation cannot reproduce the full stress-range distribution and associated cycle counting of a broad-banded sea state. For controller performance, the approximation likewise does not capture interactions among multiple simultaneously excited frequencies; consequently, the reported controller results should be interpreted within the assumptions of the frequency-domain formulation rather than as a substitute for irregular-wave time-domain control analysis. These limitations are now discussed in Section~3.3.11 ([regular-wave/JPD discussion: page/line reference]) and Section~5.3 ([limitations: page/line reference]).

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 6}{The MEEM approach is efficient for the float, but some spar and coupling coefficients are obtained from algebraic approximations, interpolation of precomputed BEM data, and corrections to ensure positive definiteness. The authors should explain how often these corrections are needed and how much they change the dynamic response or power estimates.}
\response We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. The combined effect of the spar and coupling approximations on power can be seen in Figure~36 (Appendix~C.8), which compares results using the WAMIT coefficients of WEC-Sim (top row) with results using MDOcean's own coefficients (bottom row). The remaining quantitative question is how frequently the positive-definiteness correction is activated and how much it changes the reported response. Figure~36 demonstrates the combined effect of the spar and coupling approximations, but does not isolate that correction. [TODO: insert the measured correction frequency and change in average power, and cite the corresponding Appendix~B.2 and Section~3.2.2 locations.]

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 7}{The reported runtime of 151 ms is a strong result. However, it should be made clear what is included in this runtime: a single sea state or full JPD evaluation, one design evaluation or only one module call, with or without hydrodynamic precomputation, data loading and setup costs. This is important for readers who may want to compare MDOcean with BEM, WEC-Sim or other optimization workflows.}

The reported runtime is for one complete design evaluation: all five modules, evaluated across all sea states of the site's joint probability distribution. It excludes one-time setup costs \fillin{such as loading the precomputed lookup tables (the WAMIT data interpolated for the spar and coupling coefficients, and the drag integrals)}. The float hydrodynamic coefficients are computed by MEEM within each evaluation rather than precomputed, so the runtime includes the hydrodynamic solution for every design. \fillin{We have added this description to Section 4.X (p.~X, lines~A-B). [Note: Could also state the hardware/single-core conditions.]}

\rcomment{Reviewer 4, Major Comment 8}{The present implementation is mainly demonstrated for the RM3 two-body axisymmetric point absorber. The authors should avoid implying that the framework is already generally applicable to all WEC archetypes.
